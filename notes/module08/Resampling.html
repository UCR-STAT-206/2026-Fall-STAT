Overivew

The Monte Carlo method broadly refers to the theory and practice of studying probability distributions through simulation. These methods rely on independent and identically distributed (IID) simulations from the distribution of interest. Monte Carlo works even when the modeler cannot name or write down a neat formula describing the target distribution; we need only a recipe that gives statistically valid samples from it.

Here we focus on two important resampling methods: the bootstrap and permutation tests. Both concepts can be applied to derive both point and interval estimates. We do not have enough time to cover all the theory (you should see this in other classes), but we must review a few key tools.

Theoretical Tools

Suppose \(X_1, X_2, \dots\) are IID simulations from some distribution (data), and suppose we want to know the expectation of some derived quantity from the simulations, \(Y_{i} = g(X_{i})\). Taking expectations, we have

\[ \theta \equiv E\left[ Y_1 \right] = E\left[ g(X_1) \right]. \]

This basic result provides us with the means to derive moment generating functions, higher-order moments, and even probabilities, analytically or numerically.

Under appropriate regularity conditions, statisticians have two additional tools to aid us in simulations.

Law of Large Numbers

The (strong/weak) law of large numbers (LLN) says

\[ \bar{y}_n = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n Y_i = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n g(X_i) \]

converges in probability / almost surely to \(\theta\). The result matches people’s intuitive understanding of “long-term averages” as “expected values”.

Central Limit Theorem

A central limit theorem (CLT) typically states something to the effect of

\[ \sqrt{n} (\bar{y}_n - \theta) \stackrel{d}{\rightarrow} \sigma \mbox{N}(0,1) . \]

This is a more detailed, quantitative statement. That is, for sufficiently large \(n\) a sample average is normally distributed

\[ \bar{y}_n \sim \mbox{N}( \theta , \sigma^2 / n). \]

Further, we can estimate the standard error \(\sigma / \sqrt{n}\) with \(s_n / \sqrt{n}\) where \(s_n\) is the sample standard deviation. (Recall that standard errors measure squared deviations from a “center”, typically a mean). We can also use the CLT form a confidence interval with

\[ Pr( \bar{y}_n - 1.96 s_n / \sqrt{n} < \mbox{E}[Y_1] < \bar{y}_n + 1.96 s_n / \sqrt{n} ) \approx 0.95 . \] Or we could simulate until a half-width (or width) of this confidence interval is sufficiently small, say less than \(\epsilon>0\). That is, simulate until \[ 1.96 s_n / \sqrt{n} < \epsilon . \]

Statistical Inference

Up to now, we’ve focused on obtaining estimates from models (least squares, summary statistics, optimization). The LLN and CLT are theoretical tools that from the backbone of statistical inference.

  • In probability, we are given a probabilistic model and derive conclusions/results from the model.
  • In statistics, we are given data and a statistical model (itself a family of probabilistic models) and must choose or justify choosing a particular model from the class.
    • That is, our role as data scientists/statisticians/etc is to say something useful about the data!
    • In general, we don’t know the “true” model and we’ll never know what it is. After all, the notion of “true model” may not be well-defined.
    • So we cannot say much about data with full certainty. But we can quantify how good or poor our understanding of the data is in practice.

Forms of Statistical Inferences

This is entirely from the so-called frequentist perspective.

  • Point Estimates: Statistics (i.e. functions of data) which need not be exact. They cannot be exact; we just need them to be “close” to whatever a point estimate is supposed to estimate. As such, we expect a good point estimate to be consistent, unbiased, and have reasonably low variance, among other desirable properties.

  • Interval Estimates: Think confidence intervals or regions, which are functions of statistics that do claim to capture the “true value” with some nominal probability.

  • Hypothesis Tests: These are Accept/Reject decisions about a “true”, unknown probability model. Statisticians do their best to control false positives (Type I error) and false negatives (Type II error), making tradeoffs for one type of error control depending on context.

Why do we need resampling methods?

Statistical models found in classic textbooks are “nice”: we can usually derive relatively neat, tidy formulas for statistical inference. This is not the case in general

Example: Empirical Probability

Suppose we flip a coin \(n\) times and record each instance of a “Heads” with \(X_{i} = 1\) which happens with probabilty \(p\). Otherwise, we record \(X_{i} = 0\) for the \(i\)-th coin flip. Each coin flip is independent of the others. Here the probability of “Heads”, \(p\), is unknown. We can obtain an estimate of it by

\[ E[\bar{X}] = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} X_{i} = E[X_{1}] = 0 \cdot \Pr(X_{1} = 0) + 1 \cdot \Pr(X_{1} = 1) = p \]

The LLN justifies approximating \(p\) by the first equality; i.e. the sample average of the coin flips.

function simulate_coins(n, p)
    @assert n > 0
    @assert 0 ≤ p ≤ 1

    sample_space = [0, 1]
    probs = [1-p, p]
    coin_flips = sample(sample_space, weights(probs), n; replace = true)

    num_heads = cumsum(coin_flips)
    est_heads = num_heads ./ (1:n)

    return DataFrame(coin_flip = coin_flips, num_heads = num_heads, est_heads = est_heads)
end
Random.seed!(206)
simulate_coins(10, 0.5)
n_coins = (10, 100, 500)
p = 0.5

fig = plot(xlabel = "Simulation", ylabel = "Probability", layout = grid(3, 1), legend = false)
for (k, n) in enumerate(n_coins)
    mydata = simulate_coins(n, p)
    plot!(mydata.est_heads, title = "n = $(n)", subplot = k)
    hline!([p], subplot = k, color = :red, linestyle = :dash)
end

The CLT quantifies the uncertainty in the estimate via asymptotics. We need to *repeat the process of calculating \(\hat{p}_{n}\), our estimate of \(p\) using \(n\) data points.

function simulate_empirical(n, p)
    results = simulate_coins(n, p)
    return results.est_heads[n]
end

p = 0.5

    ylabel = "Frequency",

Example: Aproximating \(\pi\)

  • The area of a circle with radius \(r\) is \(\pi r^2\).
  • If we draw a square containing that circle its area will be \(4r^2\).

So the ratio of the area of the circle to the area of the square is \[ \frac{\pi r^2}{4r^2} = \frac{\pi}{4} \]

Given this fact, we can empirically determine the ratio of the area of the circle to the area of the square we can simply multiply this number by 4 and we’ll get our approximation of \(\pi\). How?

Simulation:

  1. Randomly sample \(x\) and \(y\) values on the unit square centered at 0
  2. If \(x^2 + y^2 \le .5^2\) then the point is in the circle
  3. The ratio of points in the circle multiplied by 4 is our estimate of \(\pi\)
runs = 100_000
xs = rand(runs) .- 0.5  # Generate random x values between -0.5 and 0.5
ys = rand(runs) .- 0.5  # Generate random y values between -0.5 and 0.5
in_circle = @. xs ^ 2 + ys ^ 2 <= 0.5 ^ 2  # Check if the point is inside the circle
mc_pi = sum(in_circle) / runs * 4  # Monte Carlo estimate of π
mc_pi
scatter(xs, ys,
    markersize = 1,
    markerstrokewidth = 0,
    color = in_circle .+ 1, # Color points inside the circle in blue
    aspect_ratio = 1,
    title = "MC Approximation of Pi = $mc_pi",
    legend = false
)

Example: Integration

Let \(X \sim \Gamma (3/2, 1)\), i.e. \[ f(x) = \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}} \sqrt{x} e^{-x} 1\{x>0\} . \]

Suppose we want to find \[ \begin{aligned} \theta & = \mbox{E} \left[ \frac{1}{(X+1)\log (X+3)} \right]\\ & = \int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{(x+1)\log (x+3)} \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}} \sqrt{x} e^{-x} dx . \end{aligned} \]

  • The expectation (or integral) \(\theta\) is intractable, we don’t know how to compute it analytically.
  • Further, suppose we want to estimate this quantity such that a 95% CI length is less than 0.002.
n = 1000
x = rand(Gamma(3/2, 1), n)

y = @. 1 / ((x + 1) * log(x + 3))
est = mean(y)

mcse = std(y) / sqrt(length(y))
interval = @. est + [-1, 1] * 1.96 * mcse
interval

Sequential stopping rule

Now let’s try to obtain a CI with length less than 0.002. We’ll run our simulation using a stopping rule.

target_ci_length = 0.002
ci_length = interval[2] - interval[1]
plotting_var = [est interval[1] interval[2]]

while ci_length > target_ci_length
    new_x = rand(Gamma(3/2, 1), n)
    new_y = @. 1 / ((new_x + 1) * log(new_x + 3))
    y = hcat(y, new_y)

    est = mean(y)
    mcse = std(y) / sqrt(length(y))
    interval = @. est + [-1, 1] * 1.96 * mcse
    ci_length = interval[2] - interval[1]
    plotting_var = vcat(plotting_var, [est interval[1] interval[2]])
end
(interval, length(y))

Example: Simple Regression

Consider data \(\{(Y_{i},X_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}\) in the simple linear regression model \(Y = X\beta_{1} + \beta_{0} + \epsilon\).

  • Point Estimates: \(\hat{\beta}_{0} = \bar{Y} - \hat{\beta}_{1} \bar{X}\), \(\hat{\beta}_{1} = [\sum_{i=1}^{n} (X_{i} - \bar{X})^{2}]^{-1} \left[\sum_{i=1}^{n} (X_{i} - \bar{X}) (Y_{i} - \bar{Y})\right]\)

  • Interval Estimates: If we assume each \(\epsilon_{i}\) is normally distributed, then we learned that the statistics \(t = \frac{\hat{\beta}_{j} - \beta_{j}}{\mathrm{SE}(\hat{\beta}_{j})}\) follows a \(t\)-distribution. A symmetric \((1-\alpha)\) confidence interval for \(\hat{\beta}_{j}\) then takes the form of \(\left[\hat{\beta}_{j} - \mathrm{SE}(\hat{\beta}_{j}) t_{1-\alpha/2}, \hat{\beta}_{j} + \mathrm{SE}(\hat{\beta}_{j}) t_{1-\alpha/2}\right]\). Here the \(t_{1-\alpha/2}\) denotes the \((1-\alpha/2)\) quantile of the appropriate \(t\) distribution.

  • Hypothesis Tests: Under the same setting as above, we might be interested in checking whether \(\beta_{j}\) is meaningfully different from some reference value (say 0). That is, we might posit the null hypothesis \(H_{0}:~\beta_{j} = 0\) for a particular \(j\) under the principle of falsifiability.

Let’s create some simulation code for this basic example.

function simulate_ols_data(n::Int; b0::Real = -10, b1::Real = 2.0, noise::Real = 1.0)
    # Simulate the design matrix
    X = zeros(n, 2)
    X[:, 1] .= 1
    @views rand!(Normal(), X[:, 2])

    # Set the ground truth
    β = Float64[b0, b1]

    # Simulate the response
    y = X*β + rand(Normal(0.0, noise), n)

    return y, X, β
end

We observe only the “noisy” version of \(y_{i}\). Its theoretical mean, by definition of the simulation, ought to be \(x_{i}^{\top}\beta\). Here’s the data:

Random.seed!(206)
fig = plot(xlabel = L"Y", ylabel = "Frequency", layout = grid(3, 1))

    histogram!(yn, label = "n = $(n)", subplot = k)

If we had access to an infinite pool of data, we could simply repeat the process of fitting the \(\beta\)s and empirically calculate their standard errors. It is critical that the simulation parameters are identical to ensure we simulation data is in fact IID.

simulate() = simulate_ols_data(1000, b0 = -10.0, b1 = 2.0, noise = 1.0)

Random.seed!(206)

n_replicates = 1000
β0hat = zeros(n_replicates)
β1hat = zeros(n_replicates)

for k in 1:n_replicates
    # sample the infinite pool
    y, X, _ = simulate()

    # estimate coefficients using our estimator
    b0hat, b1hat = X \ y

    # record it
    β0hat[k] = b0hat
    β1hat[k] = b1hat
end
histogram([β0hat β1hat],
    xlabel = [L"\hat{\beta}_{0}" L"\hat{\beta}_{1}"],
    ylabel = "Frequency",
    layout = grid(1, 2),
    legend = false,
    title = "$(n_replicates) replicates",
)

  • This approach treats the “model fitting” or “estimation” as a black-box that takes data and spits out a statistic of interest. It can be quite expensive.

Big Picture

The theory of Monte Carlo is just the theory of frequentist statistical inference. The only differences are that:

  • The “data” \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) are computer simulations rather than measurements on objects in the real world.
  • The “sample size” \(n\) is the number of computer simulations rather than the size of some real world data.
  • The unknown parameter \(\theta\) is in principle completely known, given by some integral, which we are unable to do.

Everything works just the same when the data \(X_1, X_2, \dots\) are random vectors but the functions of interest \(g(X_1), g(X_2), \dots\) are scalars. However, it can be very difficult to simulate IID simulations of random variables or random vectors whose distributions are not “brand name” distributions.
