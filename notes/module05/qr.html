QR Decomposition

QR decomposition

Given \(\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}\), QR decomposition factorizes \(\mathbf{A}\) as \(\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{R}\).

  • Usually use economy QR, \(\mathbf{Q} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times r}\) where \(r\) is numerical rank.
  • \(\mathbf{Q}\) has orthonormal columns; i.e. \(\mathbf{Q}^\top \mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{I}\) (identity matrix).
  • \(\mathbf{R}\) has upper triangular structure (but not square in general).
  • Complexity: \(2np^{2} - \frac{2}{3} p^{3}\) flops; \(\mathbf{Q}\) is not stored and requires additional \(2np^{2} - \frac{2}{3} p^{3}\) flops (Householder method)
  • Less numerically stable compared to other methods for linear systems.

Gram-Schmidt Algorithm

Input: Vectors \(\mathbf{a}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{a}_k \in \mathbb{R}^n\).

Output: The Gram-Schmidt algorithm generates a sequence of orthonormal vectors \(\mathbf{q}_1, \mathbf{q}_2, \ldots\).

  • For \(i=1,\ldots,k\):

    1. Orthogonalization: \(\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_i = \mathbf{a}_i - [(\mathbf{q}_1' \mathbf{a}_i) \mathbf{q}_1 + \cdots + (\mathbf{q}_{i-1}' \mathbf{a}_i) \mathbf{q}_{i-1}]\).

    2. Test for linear independence: if \(\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_i = \mathbf{0}\), quit.

    3. Normalization: \(\mathbf{q}_i = \tilde{\mathbf{q}}_i / \|\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_i\|\).

  • If G-S does not stop early (in step 2), \(\mathbf{a}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{a}_k\) are linearly independent.

  • If G-S stops early in iteration \(i=j\), then \(\mathbf{a}_j\) is a linear combination of \(\mathbf{a}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{a}_{j-1}\) and \(\mathbf{a}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{a}_{j-1}\) are linearly independent.

Complexity of Gram-Schmidt

Computational complexity of G-S algorithm:

  • Step 1 of iteration \(i\) requires (1) \(i-1\) inner products, \(\mathbf{q}_1' \mathbf{a}_i, \ldots, \mathbf{q}_{i-1}' \mathbf{a}_i\), which costs \((i-1)(2n-1)\) flops, and (2) \(2n(i-1)\) flops to compute \(\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_i\).

  • Step 2 of iteration \(i\) requires less than \(n\) flops.

  • Step 3 of iteration \(i\) requires \(3n\) flops to normalize \(\|\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_i\|\).

  • Assuming no early termination, total computational cost of the GS algorithm to orthonormalize a set of \(k\) vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^n\) is: \[ \sum_{i=1}^k [(4n-1) (i - 1) + 3n] = (4n - 1) \frac{k(k-1)}{2} + 3nk \approx 2nk^2 = O(nk^2), \]

using \(\sum_{i=1}^k (i - 1) = k(k-1)/2\).

GS Algorithm and QR Factorization

The operations in GS can be compactly expressed as

\[ \mathbf{A} = \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{R}, \]

where \(\mathbf{A} = (\mathbf{a}_1 \, \cdots \, \mathbf{a}_k) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k}\), \(\mathbf{Q} = (\mathbf{q}_1 \, \cdots \, \mathbf{q}_k) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k}\) satisfying \(\mathbf{Q}'\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{I}_k\) (\(\mathbf{Q}\) has orthonormal columns), and \(\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times k}\) is an upper triangular matrix with positive diagonal elements.

What are the entries in \(\mathbf{R}\)? In the \(i\)-th iteration of the Gram-Schmidt algorithm

\[\begin{eqnarray*} \text{Orthogonalization step: } & & \tilde{\mathbf{q}}_i = \mathbf{a}_i - (\mathbf{q}_1' \mathbf{a}_i) \mathbf{q}_1 - \cdots - (\mathbf{q}_{i-1}' \mathbf{a}_i) \mathbf{q}_{i-1} \\ \text{Normalization step: } & & \mathbf{q}_i = \tilde{\mathbf{q}}_i / \|\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_i\|. \end{eqnarray*}\]

Therefore, \[ \mathbf{a}_i = (\mathbf{q}_1' \mathbf{a}_i) \mathbf{q}_1 + \cdots + (\mathbf{q}_{i-1}' \mathbf{a}_i) \mathbf{q}_{i-1} + \|\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_i\| \mathbf{q}_i. \]

This tells us \(R_{ij} = \mathbf{q}_i' \mathbf{a}_j\) for \(i < j\) and \(R_{ii} = \|\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_i\|\).

Thus overall Gram-Schmidt algorithm performs \[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{pmatrix} | & | & & | & | \\ \mathbf{a}_1 & \mathbf{a}_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{a}_{k-1} & \mathbf{a}_k \\ | & | & & | & | \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} | & | & & | & | \\ \mathbf{q}_1 & \mathbf{q}_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{q}_{k-1} & \mathbf{q}_k \\ | & | & & | & | \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \|\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_1\| & \mathbf{q}_1'\mathbf{a}_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{q}_1' \mathbf{a}_{k-1} & \mathbf{q}_1' \mathbf{a}_k \\ & \|\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_2\| & \cdots & \mathbf{q}_2' \mathbf{a}_{k-1} & \mathbf{q}_2' \mathbf{a}_k \\ & & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots \\ & & & \|\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_{k-1}\| & \mathbf{q}_{k-1}' \mathbf{a}_k \\ & & & & \|\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_k\| \end{pmatrix} = \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{R}. \]

Warning

The Gram-Schmidt algorithm is numerically unstable. The specific issue that arises is that the basis vectors \(\mathbf{q}_{k}\) may lose their orthogonality. The modified Gram-Schmidt is preferred. In your homework you will implement QR via Householder reflections, which is more stable and efficient (the basis vectors are computed implicitly).

Solving linear systems

Now look at solving linear systems (full column rank), assuming we already have \(\mathbf{Q}\) and \(\mathbf{R}\):

\[ \mathbf{A} \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} \equiv \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{R} \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} \implies \text{Solve}~\mathbf{R} \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{Q}^\top \mathbf{y} \]

  • Form RHS: \(2np\) flops (since \(\mathbf{Q}\) is \(n \times p\))
  • Solve with backward substitution: \(p^{2}\) flops
  • Total: \(2np + p^{2}\)

Solving least squares (regression)

Least squares problem \(\min_{\beta}~\frac{1}{2} \|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\beta\|_{2}^{2}\) has the same complexity:

  • Why? Use properties of orthonormal matrices…
  • Reduction to \(\min_{\beta}~\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{Q}^\top \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{R}\beta\|_{2}^{2}\).
  • Fit \(\beta\) (QR + solve): \(2(n-1)p^{2} - \frac{2}{3} p^{3} + 2np\)
  • Standard errors for estimates: Requires inverting \(\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}\) (one of a few cases where it is acceptable!)

We can be far more clever about inverting the matrix. View the problem of computing \((\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1}\) as:

\[ \text{Solve}~\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X} \mathbf{\Sigma} = \mathbf{I} \quad \equiv \quad \mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R} \mathbf{\Sigma} = \mathbf{I}; \]

that is, we solve two triangular systems for the matrix-valued variable \(\mathbf{\Sigma}\).

Note

Note that \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\beta})\) depends on \(\mathbf{X}\) in linear regression, but the explicit form for the standard errors depends on whatever assumptions we are willing to make about the random component (e.g. normal errors).

Example: Longley data

y = longley.Employed
X = [ones(length(y)) Matrix(select(longley, Not(:Employed)))]

Solve for coefficients:

qrX = qr(X)
β = qrX \ y

Get standard errors:

r = y - X*β # residuals
s² = r'r / (length(y) - length(β)) # correction: n - p
tmp = UpperTriangular(qrX.R)' \ (1.0 * I(length(β))) # trisolve 1
Σβ = UpperTriangular(qrX.R) \ tmp # trisolve 2
sqrt.(s² * diag(Σβ)) # variances on diagonal

Back in R:

# Regress Employed on all other variables; add intercept
fit.longley <- lm(Employed ~ . + 1, data = $longley)
# Fitted β
coef(fit.longley)
# Can see standard errors here
print(coef(summary(fit.longley)));
